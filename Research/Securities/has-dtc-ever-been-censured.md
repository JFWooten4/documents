# Has DTC ever been censured?

## Conclusion

I found no public order in which the Securities and Exchange Commission, Federal Reserve, or New York banking regulator formally **censured The Depository Trust Company (DTC)**.

That is a public-record conclusion, not proof that DTC has never received confidential examination criticism, supervisory findings, or nonpublic remedial demands.

The closest public analogue I found is the **International Power Group (IPWG) matter**, especially the period from DTC's 2009 global lock through the SEC's March 15, 2012 opinion and the 2013–2014 rulemaking that followed. In that matter the Commission formally concluded that DTC had not provided an issuer with adequate fair procedure, remanded DTC's action, and later questioned whether DTC's proposed procedural remedy complied with the Exchange Act. DTC ultimately withdrew that proposal before the Commission issued an approval or disapproval decision.

## What counts as a censure?

DTC is a registered clearing agency and therefore a self-regulatory organization for purposes of Exchange Act Section 19. Section 19(h)(1), 15 USC 78s(h)(1), expressly permits the appropriate regulatory agency to censure an SRO after notice and an opportunity for hearing when the statutory findings are met.

Source: [15 USC 78s(h), U.S. House Office of the Law Revision Counsel](https://usc-cdn.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title15-section78s).

A formal censure is therefore different from:

- an SEC order reviewing and setting aside or remanding an SRO action;
- an SEC examination deficiency or staff recommendation;
- suspension or disapproval proceedings concerning an SRO rule filing;
- a fine, censure, or other discipline that DTC itself imposes on a participant; or
- an enforcement action against DTCC, NSCC, FICC, a transfer agent, or another entity rather than DTC itself.

That distinction matters because searches for "DTC" and "censure" produce many false positives involving sanctions imposed **by** DTC or involving other DTCC subsidiaries.

## Closest episode: International Power Group

### DTC's 2009 global lock

DTC suspended clearance and settlement services for International Power Group, Ltd. common stock in 2009 after an SEC enforcement action involving persons who had received IPWG shares. DTC left custody services available but effectively imposed a global lock on the securities.

IPWG sought a hearing. DTC took the position that its hearing rule did not apply because the securities technically remained DTC-eligible while most transaction services were suspended.

### SEC opinion of March 15, 2012

In **International Power Group, Ltd., Exchange Act Release No. 34-66611 (Mar. 15, 2012)**, the Commission rejected DTC's narrow view of the issuer's procedural rights.

The Commission concluded that IPWG was entitled to Commission review of DTC's limitation of services and that **DTC did not provide IPWG with adequate fair procedure**. It remanded the matter to DTC for development of a record and further consideration under procedures consistent with the Exchange Act.

The Commission also explained that DTC could act quickly to avert imminent harm, but could not maintain a suspension indefinitely without expedited fair process for the affected issuer.

Primary source: [SEC Opinion, International Power Group, Ltd., Release 34-66611](https://www.sec.gov/file/34-66611pdf).

SEC contemporaneous summary: [SEC News Digest, March 15, 2012](https://www.sec.gov/news/digest/2012/dig031512.htm).

### Why this was still not a censure

The International Power opinion was an adjudicatory review of DTC's restriction of access to services, not a Section 19(h) enforcement order sanctioning DTC.

The Commission did not order that "DTC is censured," did not impose a civil penalty on DTC, and did not issue a cease-and-desist order against DTC. Instead, it found the procedures inadequate and remanded the underlying DTC action.

So International Power is best described as an **adverse Commission determination about DTC's compliance with fair-procedure requirements**, not a formal censure.

## The 2013–2014 aftermath

International Power did not end with the 2012 opinion. DTC later filed **SR-DTC-2013-11** to establish procedures for issuers when DTC imposes or intends to impose restrictions on deposits or book-entry transfers.

The SEC did not simply approve the filing. On March 19, 2014, it instituted proceedings to determine whether to approve or disapprove the proposal. The proceeding identified questions under Exchange Act Sections 17A(b)(3)(H) and 17A(b)(5), including whether the proposal provided affected issuers the required fair procedure, notice, opportunity to be heard, and record.

The Commission later extended its time for action. DTC then withdrew SR-DTC-2013-11 in August 2014 before a final approval or disapproval order.

Primary docket and releases: [SEC, SR-DTC-2013-11](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/self-regulatory-organization-rulemaking/sr-dtc-2013-11).

This sequence is important because it shows the 2012 ruling was not merely rhetorical. DTC tried to codify a remedy, the SEC subjected that remedy to possible-disapproval proceedings, and the proposal did not reach approval.

The public record does **not** establish why DTC withdrew the filing, so the withdrawal should not be described as an admission or as proof the SEC was about to disapprove it.

## Earlier examination criticism: 1985

The SEC's 1985 Annual Report records a staff review of the member-monitoring and risk-assessment programs of DTC and three other depositories. The inspections identified "some weakness" in their risk-assessment programs.

SEC staff recommended formal risk assessments reported to the depositories' boards and a task force to develop consistent programs addressing common risk.

Source: [SEC Annual Report 1985](https://www.sec.gov/about/annual_report/1985.pdf).

This is genuine regulatory criticism of DTC, but it is materially farther from a censure than International Power. It appears as an examination finding and staff recommendation, not an adjudicated violation, public enforcement proceeding, cease-and-desist order, monetary sanction, or censure.

## Current rulemaking intervention: SR-DTC-2026-007

In **SR-DTC-2026-007**, DTC filed an immediately effective change to its Operational Arrangements. On July 9, 2026, the SEC temporarily suspended the change and instituted proceedings to determine whether it should be approved or disapproved.

Source: [SEC, SR-DTC-2026-007](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/self-regulatory-organization-rulemaking/sr-dtc-2026-007).

As of **October 7, 2026**, the SEC's DTC rulemaking page still lists the May 19 filing and July 9 suspension/order-instituting-proceedings for SR-DTC-2026-007, with no later final approval or disapproval disposition shown for that file number.

Current DTC docket: [SEC DTC self-regulatory organization rulemaking](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/self-regulatory-organization-rulemaking/dtc).

This is significant regulatory intervention, but it is still a rule-filing review rather than an enforcement finding that DTC violated the Exchange Act. It is therefore not as close to censure as International Power.

## Comparator: FICC was actually censured

The strongest comparison is **Fixed Income Clearing Corporation (FICC)**, another DTCC clearing-agency subsidiary.

In **Exchange Act Release No. 34-93458 (Oct. 28, 2021)**, the SEC instituted settled proceedings under Sections 19(h) and 21C. The Commission ordered FICC to cease and desist from specified violations, imposed an $8 million civil penalty and undertakings, and expressly ordered that **FICC is censured**.

Primary source: [SEC Order, FICC, Release 34-93458](https://www.sec.gov/file/34-93458pdf).

This comparator matters because it shows that Section 19(h) censure is not merely theoretical for systemically important DTCC clearing subsidiaries. The SEC has used that authority against a DTC corporate sibling.

The contrast is useful:

| DTC — International Power | FICC — 2021 |
| --- | --- |
| SEC reviewed DTC's restriction of services | SEC brought a settled enforcement proceeding |
| SEC found inadequate fair procedure | SEC found specified Exchange Act/rule violations |
| Matter remanded to DTC | Cease-and-desist order imposed |
| No DTC civil penalty | $8 million civil penalty |
| No order stating "DTC is censured" | Order expressly states "FICC is censured" |

That makes it difficult to characterize International Power itself as a censure while also showing why it is DTC's closest known public analogue.

## Comparator: Philadelphia Depository Trust Company

The SEC has also brought public Section 19(h) and 21C proceedings against another securities depository.

In 1997, the Commission instituted administrative and cease-and-desist proceedings against the **Philadelphia Depository Trust Company (Philadep)** and the Stock Clearing Corporation of Philadelphia. The SEC found failures to comply with their own rules and procedures, failures to file required proposed rule changes, and control failures involving protected funds. The entities consented to cease-and-desist relief and undertakings.

Sources:

- [SEC Annual Report 1997](https://www.sec.gov/files/1997.pdf)
- [SEC history of Philadep withdrawal and 1997 proceeding](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/2002/11/philadelphia-depository-trust-company-notice-request-comment-order-granting-request-withdrawal)

Again, this was **not DTC**. It is useful because it shows what a public enforcement trajectory against a securities depository looks like.

## Supervisory context and the limits of the public record

DTC is simultaneously:

- a clearing agency registered with the SEC;
- a New York limited-purpose trust company; and
- a member of the Federal Reserve System.

For Title VIII purposes, the SEC has primary jurisdiction over DTC as its Supervisory Agency. NYDFS supervises DTC as a New York-chartered trust company, and the Federal Reserve supervises DTC as a state member bank and coordinates with the SEC and NYDFS.

Source: [Federal Reserve — Designated Financial Market Utilities: DTC](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/designated_fmu_about.htm).

That overlapping supervision means a search of public SEC enforcement materials cannot prove that DTC has never received confidential supervisory criticism from any regulator. Bank and FMU examinations can generate nonpublic findings.

The defensible formulation is therefore:

> **No public formal censure of DTC was found. International Power Group, particularly the 2012 Commission opinion and 2013–2014 remedial rulemaking, appears to be the closest public episode.**

## Ranking the closest public episodes

1. **International Power Group / SR-DTC-2013-11 (2009–2014)** — closest. The SEC formally found DTC's procedures inadequate, remanded the matter, and later subjected DTC's proposed procedural response to possible-disapproval proceedings.
2. **1985 SEC depository examination** — direct examination criticism and remedial recommendations, but no public enforcement finding.
3. **SR-DTC-2026-007 (2026)** — SEC suspension of an immediately effective DTC rule filing and institution of approval/disapproval proceedings, but no finding of misconduct or violation.
4. **Other adverse DTC rule-filing outcomes** — potentially important as regulatory pushback, but categorically different from a Section 19(h) sanction unless tied to an enforcement finding.

## Research note

This review searched for public SEC material using combinations of DTC's full legal name with terms such as *censure*, *censured*, *cease-and-desist*, enforcement, examination, sanction, and Section 19(h), and compared the results against known enforcement actions involving FICC and other securities depositories.

The negative conclusion should be revisited if an older administrative order, Federal Reserve enforcement action, NYDFS order, or archival SEC examination material surfaces that is not indexed in the modern public databases.
