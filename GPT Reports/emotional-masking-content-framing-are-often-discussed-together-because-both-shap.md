# Emotional Masking and Content Framing

## Executive summary

Emotional masking and content framing are often discussed together because both shape social outcomes without necessarily changing the underlying reality. But they operate at different levels. Emotional masking concerns how a person regulates or disguises emotional expression in order to fit display rules, avoid stigma, manage impressions, or reduce interpersonal risk. In current research literatures, its major forms include expressive suppression, surface acting and deep acting in emotional labor, and camouflaging/masking/assimilation/compensation in autism research. Content framing, by contrast, concerns how information is selected, valenced, sequenced, and made salient so that audiences interpret, evaluate, or choose differently, even when the underlying facts are equivalent or only selectively emphasized.[^1]

Theoretical mechanisms differ accordingly. Emotional masking is principally a self-regulation and impression-management process: it changes the actor's outward signals, often under social or institutional pressure. Content framing is principally an audience-facing cognitive-communicative process: it changes salience, reference points, perceived applicability, or evaluative interpretation of content. In short: masking modifies the display of the self; framing modifies the presentation of the message. Priming sits nearby but is not identical: communication theory generally treats priming as shifting what concepts are readily accessible, whereas framing shapes which considerations appear relevant or applicable to interpretation.[^2]

Empirically, the two processes produce different profiles of benefit and harm. Emotional masking can improve short-term coordination, service performance, conflict management, safety, and social acceptance, but sustained suppression and surface acting are repeatedly associated with greater strain, worse memory for emotional material, disrupted social interaction, emotional exhaustion, lower authenticity, and—in autistic masking research—higher internalizing symptoms and burnout risk. Framing effects, meanwhile, are real but heterogeneous: they can alter medical choices, risk understanding, policy opinions, product evaluations, vaccination intentions, smoking intentions, privacy disclosure, and cookie-consent behavior, although effect sizes are often context-dependent and can shrink under competing frames, richer information, or direct experience.[^3]

For practitioners, the central design problem is not "which one works," but what kind of influence is morally justified for the task at hand. Masking should not be treated as a free organizational resource, nor as evidence that someone is "coping fine." Framing should not be reduced to click-through optimization or attitude change alone. The strongest evidence supports approaches that preserve comprehension, trust, and agency: reduce chronic masking demands; measure well-being, not only compliance; present risks transparently; test frames on understanding and trust, not only persuasion; and avoid dark-pattern tactics that exploit salience while undermining informed choice.[^4]

## Assumptions and scope

This report assumes that "emotional masking" is a useful analytic umbrella rather than a single settled technical term. Under that umbrella, I include expressive suppression, emotional-labor strategies such as surface acting, and social camouflaging in autism research because these literatures all concern the strategic alteration of outward emotional or interpersonal presentation under social constraints. I also assume that "content framing" is broader than gain-versus-loss wording alone, and includes equivalence framing, attribute framing, goal framing, emphasis framing, and closely related presentation and priming effects in communication research. I do not use "masking" here in the signal-processing sense, nor "framing" in the purely visual-design or construction sense.[^5]

A second assumption is that these literatures are partially overlapping but not interchangeable. Emotional masking research usually starts from emotion regulation, impression management, stigma, occupational display rules, or neurodiversity. Framing research usually starts from judgment and decision-making, media effects, persuasion, strategic communication, or risk communication. Where I compare them directly, I am making a synthesis across fields rather than claiming that the original authors treated them as one unified construct.[^6]

### Limitations

The evidence base is uneven. Emotional masking has stronger validated self-report instruments than content framing does, while framing has stronger randomized experimental traditions than masking does. HCI evidence on framing is newer and more mixed than the classic health and judgment-and-decision literatures, and research on autistic camouflaging remains conceptually active, with open debate about whether "camouflaging" should be modeled as a special case of broader impression management or as a distinct construct.[^7]

## Definitions and distinctions

Emotional masking refers to the regulation, concealment, or substitution of outward emotional or social signals so that observers see something different from the actor's spontaneous state. In emotion-regulation research, this includes expressive suppression, a response-focused strategy that inhibits visible emotional output. In emotional-labor research, it includes surface acting, where people alter expression without changing underlying feeling, and deep acting, where they try to change felt emotion so display becomes more congruent. In autism research, it includes camouflaging, usually parsed into compensation, masking, and assimilation strategies. In self-presentation research, it aligns with higher self-monitoring: observing situational cues and adjusting public behavior for appropriateness or advantage.[^8]

Content framing refers to presenting information so that some considerations become more salient, more memorable, or more normatively compelling than others. In classic decision research, a framing effect occurs when logically equivalent descriptions, such as gains versus losses, alter choices. In consumer and health communication research, scholars often distinguish attribute framing ("75% lean" versus "25% fat"), goal framing (benefits of compliance versus costs of noncompliance), and risky-choice framing. In communication studies, framing is broader still: it concerns how communicators select and emphasize aspects of reality in ways that promote problem definitions, causal interpretations, moral evaluations, or preferred remedies.[^9]

The core distinction is therefore where the intervention sits in the causal chain. Emotional masking intervenes inside the actor's expression system: what I feel, what I show, and how much dissonance or effort is required to keep them apart. Content framing intervenes inside the audience's interpretation system: what seems salient, risky, attractive, fair, or actionable about the message. Masking is typically evaluated through well-being, authenticity, social interaction quality, or performance strain; framing is typically evaluated through judgment, preference, recall, persuasion, understanding, or behavior.[^10]

The two also differ normatively. Emotional masking usually redistributes costs toward the person doing the masking; framing usually redistributes influence toward the person or institution designing the message. That is why masking literatures foreground burnout, stigma, and authenticity, whereas framing literatures foreground persuasion, competence, transparency, and autonomy.[^11]

## Mechanisms and theory

At the level of emotional masking, the dominant framework is Gross's process model of emotion regulation. On that account, strategies that act early in the emotion-generation process differ from strategies that act late. Suppression is a late, response-focused strategy: the emotional response has already formed, and the person is now modulating its outward display. This helps explain why suppression often preserves visible composure while leaving physiological activation, memory cost, or social strain relatively intact. Emotional-labor models extend this logic into workplace settings: surface acting alters expression while preserving inner feeling, whereas deep acting attempts to reduce emotional dissonance by changing appraisal or feeling itself.[^13]

This distinction is not merely terminological. Experimental work on suppression found that keeping one's emotional behavior from showing can disrupt communication and elevate stress in social interaction, while classic research by Richards and Gross showed cognitive costs of "keeping one's cool," including reduced memory for emotional information under suppression conditions. Longitudinal and meta-analytic work in occupational settings likewise shows that surface acting is the more consistent predictor of later strain, whereas deep acting is less uniformly harmful and in some contexts can be associated with better service performance.[^14]

Camouflaging in autism adds another layer. Ai, Cunningham, and Lai argue that camouflaging can be profitably reconsidered as a form of transactional impression management, but one shaped by neurotype mismatch, stigma, and the need to survive in predominantly non-autistic environments. This matters because the same outward behavior may arise from very different motivations: safety, belonging, employment, diagnostic avoidance, conflict prevention, or self-protection. Hull's CAT-Q framework captures three common modes—compensation, masking, and assimilation—showing that "masking" is not one thing but a cluster of strategies.[^15]

A key theoretical consequence follows: emotional masking is not always pathological or irrational. In some settings it is adaptive, socially normative, or professionally expected. The literature on expressive suppression also reports that social consequences vary by relational and cultural context, and meta-analytic work suggests that links between suppression and positive affect are culturally moderated. So the right comparison is not "masking versus authenticity" in the abstract, but what kind of masking, for whom, under what constraints, and with what cumulative load.[^16]

Content framing rests on a different family of mechanisms. The classic judgment-and-decision account comes from Tversky and Kahneman: preferences are often reference-dependent, so equivalent outcomes framed as gains or losses can trigger different risk preferences. Subsequent work by Levin, Schneider, and Gaeth clarified that not all frames are alike; attribute, goal, and risky-choice frames operate through somewhat different pathways. Communication theory broadened the concept beyond equivalence: Entman's influential formulation treats framing as selecting and highlighting aspects of reality so as to promote particular problem definitions, causal interpretations, evaluations, or remedies.[^17]

Within communication research, framing is often distinguished from priming through the accessibility versus applicability distinction. Agenda setting and priming largely concern what comes readily to mind; framing concerns how a message activates interpretive schemas that seem relevant or fitting to the issue. Scheufele's process model further separates frame building from frame setting, making clear that frames are both produced by institutions and internalized by audiences. Competitive-framing research then adds an important qualification: frames do not operate in a vacuum. Their effects depend on the quality of the frame, the audience's prior commitments, and whether rival frames are available.[^18]

One useful way to summarize the difference is this: emotional masking changes signal expression under constraint; content framing changes signal interpretation under selection and salience. That is why masking research repeatedly returns to emotional dissonance, authenticity, and exhaustion, whereas framing research repeatedly returns to salience, valence, comprehension, competition, and choice architecture.[^19]

## Empirical evidence and case studies

### Social psychology and interpersonal interaction

The suppression literature shows fairly consistent short-run trade-offs. In laboratory interaction studies, expressive suppression impaired social communication and increased stress-related costs relative to more natural responding. In the cognitive literature, suppression produced memory costs. At the dispositional level, Gross and John found that stable differences in suppression and reappraisal relate to affect, social relationships, and well-being, and a later meta-analysis covering more than 21,000 participants found suppression linked to worse mental-health indicators while reappraisal showed the opposite pattern.[^20]

This matters analytically because emotional masking is often pursued for interpersonal smoothness, yet the evidence suggests that concealment can degrade the very interactional quality it is meant to protect. When observers receive a flattened or effortfully controlled signal, the result may be lower attunement, weaker trust, and reduced memory or coordination rather than "true neutrality." That is an especially important point for teamwork, clinical encounters, and intimate contexts.[^21]

### Work, service, and management

In workplaces, emotional masking is most systematically studied as emotional labor. Grandey's classic service-work paper found that surface acting was more detrimental than deep acting for both emotional exhaustion and peer-rated service delivery. Hülsheger and Schewe's meta-analysis of ninety-five independent studies concluded that emotional labor dimensions are linked to both well-being and performance outcomes, with surface acting and emotion-rule dissonance carrying the strongest costs. Longitudinal work likewise found that surface acting predicted later strain, whereas deep acting predicted later job performance.[^22]

The healthcare literature largely converges with that pattern. Reviews and nursing studies report that surface acting and emotional dissonance are associated with stress, burnout, and degraded job satisfaction, while resilience, psychological safety, and organizational support appear to buffer these effects. A recent nursing study also found that display rules affected caring behaviors and emotional exhaustion through surface and deep acting, which is precisely the kind of mediated pathway practitioners often ignore when they treat "professional demeanor" as costless.[^23]

A practical case study is mindfulness training at work. Hülsheger and colleagues reported that the causal effect of mindfulness self-training on emotional exhaustion was mediated by reduced surface acting, suggesting that interventions can work not by demanding better masking, but by reducing the reliance on costly masking strategies in the first place.[^24]

### Autism, stigma, and clinical mental health

Autistic camouflaging is one of the clearest contemporary cases where emotional masking cannot be interpreted simply as "social skill." Hull and colleagues developed the CAT-Q from autistic adults' own experiences and found a three-factor structure: compensation, masking, and assimilation. Earlier work had already identified social camouflaging as a common coping strategy in autistic adults, and later reviews describe it as often socially motivated but linked to overlooked need, burnout, identity strain, and low self-esteem.[^25]

Recent evidence strengthens that concern. Ross and colleagues reported that camouflaging significantly predicted internalizing symptoms when controlling for autistic traits. Evans and colleagues linked higher autistic masking with worse mental health, authenticity challenges, and burnout. At the conceptual level, Ai and colleagues' "transactional impression management" account helps explain why masking may provide immediate social adaptation while simultaneously increasing long-run psychosocial burden.[^26]

The clinical implication is straightforward but often missed: outward social smoothness can be diagnostically misleading. Camouflaging may delay recognition of support needs, particularly when high social effort is mistaken for low impairment. That is why recent reviews call for support models that do not treat successful masking as proof of well-being.[^27]

### Communication studies and public opinion

Communication studies broaden the picture from individual choice to public discourse. Entman's framing paradigm emphasizes that framing works by selection and salience. Scheufele's model formalized frame building, frame setting, and feedback between journalists and audiences. Competitive-framing research then showed that framing effects are shaped by the interaction of audience traits, frame quality, and communication environments; competition changes framing effects but does not eliminate them.[^28]

The most cautious current synthesis is the meta-analysis by Amsalem and Zoizner, whose title says it well: framing effects in politics are real, but limited. This is an important corrective. Framing is neither illusion nor omnipotence. It matters, but its effects are conditional on issue domain, frame type, competition, and prior commitments. That conclusion should temper both overconfidence in strategic communication and blanket dismissals of framing research.[^29]

### Marketing and consumer behavior

Consumer research provides one of the cleanest demonstrations of content framing. Levin and Gaeth's ground-beef study found that consumers evaluated beef more favorably when it was labeled "75% lean" rather than "25% fat," and that the effect weakened after consumers actually tasted the product. That attenuation is theoretically important: framing often matters most when direct diagnostic experience is weak or absent.[^30]

Health-related consumer messaging shows a similar but more variable pattern. O'Keefe and Jensen's meta-analysis of ninety-three studies found that gain-framed prevention messages were statistically more persuasive than loss-framed ones overall, but the effect was small and driven heavily by dental-hygiene studies. Gallagher and Updegraff's later meta-analysis similarly concluded that gain frames appear more effective for prevention behaviors, while calling for closer attention to context. These are not "one frame always wins" results; they are context-sensitive persuasion effects.[^31]

Specific message studies show how framing can spill over from evaluation to policy support. Bigman, Cappella, and Hornik found that presenting the HPV vaccine as 70% effective rather than 30% ineffective increased perceived effectiveness and policy support for vaccine mandates. Abhyankar and colleagues found a loss-frame advantage for MMR vaccination intentions in one experimental context. Popova and colleagues found that different ways of framing very-low-nicotine cigarettes affected quitting intentions and risk perceptions among adult smokers.[^32]

### HCI, privacy, and user interfaces

HCI evidence is especially informative because it exposes the thin line between helpful framing and manipulative interface design. In a CHI extended abstract on cookie consent, Ma and Birrell applied prospect-theoretic framing to banner design, extending framing research into consent interfaces. More fully reported evidence comes from Bongard-Blanchy and colleagues' EuroUSEC experiment with 848 participants: negative framing increased app-permission acceptance, while positive framing could reduce acceptance when users did not understand why the permission was needed. The same study found no significant overall framing effect on perceived informedness, and only partial trust effects, underscoring that disclosure behavior and perceived transparency do not necessarily move together.[^33]

HCI also supplies counterevidence against easy assumptions. Gluck and colleagues found that short-form privacy notices could raise awareness of practices, but they did not find an effect from positive versus negative framing in their notice conditions. That is a consequential result: some interface problems are not solvable by valence shifts alone, and clearer structure may matter more than persuasive wording. Schaub and colleagues' design-space work similarly emphasizes timing, modality, control, and context as core variables for effective privacy notices.[^34]

Taken together, these HCI studies suggest a domain-specific lesson: content framing is powerful enough to alter disclosure, but not reliably enough to substitute for genuine usability and meaningful consent architecture. That is why framing in HCI must be evaluated alongside trust, informedness, and user control rather than treated as a pure conversion lever.[^35]

### Clinical communication and patient decision-making

Clinical contexts make the stakes especially visible. In classic treatment-choice work, McNeil and colleagues showed that how alternatives were presented could influence preferences between therapies. Later randomized work by Armstrong and colleagues found that framing risk graphically as mortality rather than survival reduced understanding and decreased interest in preventive surgery, with stronger effects among less educated and non-White participants. Edwards and colleagues' review likewise found that the presentation of risk and benefit information affects knowledge, risk perception, anxiety, and intentions.[^36]

Research on communicating benefit magnitude also matters here. In one study of cardiovascular prevention, most patients preferred graphic presentations over numeric formats, and ninety percent preferred positive over negative framing when asked directly. Gigerenzer and colleagues argue, however, that preference is not enough: transparent risk communication requires formats that improve genuine statistical understanding, especially frequency statements and representations that make denominators clear. Gaissmaier and Gigerenzer go further, warning that collective statistical illiteracy undermines truly informed shared decision-making.[^37]

The upshot is that clinical framing should be judged by a higher standard than marketing framing. The question is not only whether a frame moves intentions, but whether it improves understanding without covertly steering choices away from patient values.[^38]

## Measurement and evaluation

Emotional masking has a relatively mature measurement toolkit. The Emotional Labour Scale developed by Brotheridge and Lee is a 15-item self-report measure covering six facets of workplace emotional display, including frequency, intensity, variety, duration, and both surface and deep acting. The Emotion Regulation Questionnaire is a 10-item measure of two broadly used strategies, cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression. The original Self-Monitoring Scale and the revised Lennox and Wolfe version measure individual differences in monitoring and adjusting expressive behavior to social cues. For autistic masking, Hull and colleagues' CAT-Q remains the most widely used self-report measure, with 25 items organized around compensation, masking, and assimilation.[^39]

Those instruments should not be used in isolation. The masking literature repeatedly benefits from multi-method assessment: self-report, diary or experience-sampling methods, peer ratings, behavioral coding, and physiological or task-based measures. Grandey's service work study used peer-rated service delivery alongside emotional-exhaustion outcomes; suppression studies assessed downstream social and cognitive effects rather than relying on self-description alone. This is a strong methodological lesson: if the construct concerns discrepant outward display, then observer-based and performance-based indicators are often essential.[^40]

Framing research is measured differently because the constructs are usually experimental manipulations rather than stable individual differences. Standard methods include randomized assignment to logically equivalent or strategically contrasted frames; factorial designs that vary frame valence, risk level, source, or format; and outcomes such as categorical choice, policy support, perceived risk, behavioral intention, recall, trust, and comprehension. Bigman's HPV experiment measured perceived effectiveness and policy support; Popova's nicotine study measured anticipated behavior and harm perception; Bongard-Blanchy's permission-request study measured disclosure, trustworthiness, and informedness.[^41]

For high-stakes framing, especially in health and HCI, the best evaluation protocols do more than test "which frame performs best." They also test manipulation validity, understanding, equity of comprehension across subgroups, trust, and downstream behavior. Armstrong's survival/mortality-curve work explicitly measured understanding. Edwards' review centers patient knowledge, anxiety, risk perception, and behavior. Privacy-notice research similarly evaluates awareness, comprehension, and control rather than relying only on acceptance rates.[^42]

A useful rule is this: evaluate masking with well-being, strain, authenticity, relational quality, and performance metrics; evaluate framing with comprehension, fairness, trust, behavioral choice, robustness under competing frames, and subgroup sensitivity. If a practitioner measures only service politeness in masking or only click-through in framing, the likely result is an overestimate of benefit and an underestimate of harm.[^43]

## Goals, trade-offs, and ethics

Emotional masking and content framing are both tools of social navigation, which is exactly why they generate ethical ambiguity. Emotional masking can be protective. It may help a clinician remain composed during crisis, an employee remain professional under abuse, or an autistic person reduce immediate stigma risk. Yet the literature consistently warns that chronic, externally demanded masking can lead to emotional exhaustion, poorer well-being, authenticity conflict, or burnout. In autism research, masking may also obscure support needs and intensify minority-stress pathways.[^44]

Content framing is similarly double-edged. It can improve communication by matching message design to risk structure, audience comprehension, or the decision task. But it can also function as covert steering—particularly when it exploits fear, obscures denominators, withholds relevant counterframes, or uses interface design to increase disclosure without increasing understanding. In privacy interfaces, that concern overlaps with the dark-pattern literature. In health communication, it overlaps with misinformation and trust. The ethical fault line is therefore not the existence of framing—some framing is unavoidable—but whether the framing is transparent, proportionate, reversible, and accountable to the audience's interests rather than merely the persuader's conversion metric.[^45]

There is also an autonomy issue. If a choice architect celebrates a frame because it raises uptake while simultaneously lowering understanding, the intervention may be behaviorally effective yet normatively weak. Armstrong's mortality-curve findings, Gigerenzer's work on statistical literacy, and the clinical risk-communication literature together imply that some "effective" presentations are ethically inferior because they impair informed understanding or differentially disadvantage people with lower health literacy.[^46]

Trust is a common downstream variable across both literatures. Chronic masking can make others feel distance even while preserving politeness; manipulative framing can increase short-run compliance while degrading institutional credibility when audiences later recognize the steering attempt. Contemporary public-health literature on misinformation emphasizes that trust must be built across informational, interpersonal, and institutional levels—not squeezed out of narrow message optimization.[^47]

## Best practices and common pitfalls

For managers and organizations, the most evidence-aligned approach is to reduce unnecessary masking demands rather than simply training employees to do it better. Where some emotional regulation is unavoidable, organizations should distinguish brief composure from chronic surface acting; provide recovery time, autonomy, support, and psychologically safe climates; and evaluate well-being alongside service quality. Mindfulness and related interventions can help when they reduce reliance on costly surface acting, but they are not substitutes for humane display rules or safe staffing.[^48]

For clinicians, especially in mental-health and autism-related contexts, the best practice is to treat polished presentation as potentially effortful rather than presumptively healthy. Use masking-sensitive assessment, do not infer low need from high camouflage, and be cautious about reinforcing masking as a default treatment goal. When discussing treatment risks or benefits, favor transparent presentations—absolute risks, clear denominators, frequencies, and visual aids that improve comprehension rather than mortality-heavy or selectively valenced formats that steer by confusion.[^49]

For communicators, public-health professionals, and marketers, frame selection should be justified by the communication objective and subject to pretesting. Test not only attitude change or behavioral intent, but comprehension, trust, subgroup differences, and resistance under competing frames. In preventive-health communication, gains often perform slightly better overall, but not universally. In some vaccination or cessation contexts, loss frames or alternative formulations work better. The right operational question is therefore "which frame best serves an informed, durable response for this audience and this behavior," not "which frame wins the A/B test fastest."[^50]

For designers and HCI practitioners, avoid treating framing as a substitute for clarity. If a privacy notice or permission request produces more agreement but no increase in perceived informedness or trust, that should count as a design warning, not a success. Privacy notice research supports attention to timing, context, control, and concise structuring; framing can help or hurt at the margin, but it does not erase the need for intelligible consent architecture.[^35]

The most common pitfalls are strikingly consistent across domains: conflating outward smoothness with internal well-being; treating a frame that changes behavior as automatically "better"; ignoring subgroup differences in understanding; rewarding strategic self-concealment as if it were free labor; and optimizing for uptake while neglecting trust, authenticity, or informed choice.[^51]
