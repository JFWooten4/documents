# Presentation and Masking Across Domains

## Executive summary

This report treats presentation as the intentional structuring, encoding, or rendering of information so that an intended observer can detect it, understand it, and act on it more effectively, and masking as the intentional or incidental reduction of a target's detectability, discriminability, or identifiability for some observer or task. That framing is consistent with work on information transfer and perception, graphical encoding, accessibility, psychoacoustics, de-identification, and social self-presentation. Across domains, the key difference is not the medium but the optimization target: presentation tries to maximize useful access; masking tries to reduce, redirect, or condition access.[^1]

The most important analytical result is that presentation and masking are not simple opposites. In practice, they often co-occur. A visual scrim masks the background to present a dialog more clearly; office sound masking presents controlled noise to conceal intelligible speech; format-preserving encryption preserves presentation-level form while concealing value; progressive disclosure hides complexity in order to improve comprehension; social self-presentation can require masking emotion or identity cues. In other words, many real systems are hybrids that choose what to reveal, to whom, and under what conditions.[^2]

Across visual design, audio/speech, user interfaces, data privacy, and social/psychological contexts, the recurring trade-off is clarity versus concealment, but that trade-off is rarely binary. The better question is: clarity for which observer, concealment from which observer, at what cost, and measured how? In accessible design, low contrast or hidden structure may be aesthetically subtle yet functionally exclusionary. In privacy, superficial masking may look safe while leaving re-identification risk high. In workplaces, sound masking may reduce distraction and privacy risk but also increase annoyance if poorly tuned. In social contexts, masking may protect against stigma or conflict while increasing fatigue, emotional dissonance, or reduced well-being.[^3]

Evaluation therefore has to be task-specific and observer-specific. Visual presentation is assessed with comprehension accuracy, contrast ratios, and response times; perceptual masking with detection thresholds and recognition latency; speech presentation with intelligibility measures such as SII or STI; speech privacy masking with SNR, STI, distraction distance, or SPC; privacy masking with formal guarantees such as k or ε plus empirical re-identification tests and utility loss; social masking with validated scales such as the Self-Monitoring Scale, the Emotional Labour Scale, and the CAT-Q together with downstream outcomes such as burnout, anxiety, depression, and self-esteem.[^4]

The report's practical recommendation is straightforward: do not ask whether to "present" or to "mask" in the abstract. First define the information asset, the intended and unintended observers, the desired task outcome, and the acceptable cost in usability, accessibility, utility, and ethics. Then select techniques that make those trade-offs explicit and measurable.[^5]

## Conceptual framework

A cross-domain way to understand presentation and masking is to model any system as a relation among signal, noise, observer, and task. Information-theoretic and signal-detection traditions both ask how well an observer can distinguish a signal from uncertainty or interference. In that sense, presentation is an intervention that improves effective signal quality, structure, or interpretability for the intended task, while masking is an intervention that raises threshold, reduces discriminability, or alters identifiability. Psychoacoustic and visual masking are literal perceptual cases of this logic; accessibility standards, privacy standards, and self-presentation theories are higher-level social or technical versions of the same problem.[^6]

In visual and UI contexts, presentation works by improving salience, hierarchy, and semantic recoverability. WCAG formalizes this idea when it requires information, structure, and relationships conveyed through presentation to remain programmatically determinable and when it requires adequate contrast so content can still be perceived by users with varying abilities. In data visualization, Cleveland and McGill showed that encoding choice itself changes how accurately people can extract quantitative information; position on a common scale is far more reliable than angle or area for precise judgments.[^7]

In masking, the mechanism is different. A mask can work by competition, substitution, threshold elevation, added noise, reduced precision, suppression, or redirected attention. In visual masking, a target's visibility falls when a mask appears in close spatiotemporal proximity; in auditory masking, a stronger sound can render weaker nearby components inaudible; in privacy, quasi-identifiers or unique codes can be generalized, removed, or protected with formal noise addition and governance; in social life, individuals may reduce the readability of feelings or identity cues through surface acting, role management, or camouflaging.[^8]

A useful operational distinction is this: presentation optimizes for successful access, whereas masking optimizes for restricted, deferred, or selective access. That distinction also explains why the same technique can flip categories depending on the observer. A password field is masking from shoulder surfers but presentation for the legitimate user if it supports autofill, paste, accessibility labels, and error prevention. A dialog scrim masks background content but presents the active task. Differential privacy masks individuals while presenting population-level statistics. Social smiling can present affiliation while masking strain.[^9]

One important implication follows from this framework: presentation failures and masking failures can look similar at the surface. Low contrast, excessive translucency, noisy audio, over-generalized data, or emotional suppression can all make information "disappear," but for very different reasons and with different ethical valence. That is why rigorous analysis needs both mechanism and purpose, not just appearance.[^10]

## Domain analyses

Visual design. In visual design, presentation is fundamentally about choosing encodings that align with human perceptual strengths. Cleveland and McGill's classic experiments in graphical perception found that judgments based on position along a common scale outperform judgments based on angle or subtended area; in one of their reported comparisons, pie charts were more accurate than bars in only 3 of 40 cases. Official accessibility guidance complements that finding by specifying perceptual minimums: WCAG requires information and relationships conveyed visually to remain recoverable in code or text, requires at least 4.5:1 contrast for ordinary text, and requires 3:1 contrast for meaningful non-text visual cues. Google's automotive guidance similarly recommends using color minimally and purposefully, using transparency and opacity to guide visual focus, and maintaining at least 4.5:1 contrast.[^11]

Visual masking in the stricter perceptual sense occurs when a mask reduces the visibility of a target shown nearby in time or space. Reviews of visual masking define it as a case where a mask reduces the visibility of another stimulus in close spatiotemporal vicinity; metacontrast is a specific case where a brief target is followed by a spatially adjacent mask, and object substitution masking is a further case associated with unattended targets. In design practice, a less literal but widely used descendant of this logic is the scrim: a semi-opaque layer that obscures the background behind a dialog or notification so the active element gains priority. This can be beneficial, but WCAG's "Focus Not Obscured" criterion makes clear that author-created overlays, sticky banners, or semi-opaque layers become a failure when they hide the focused element or its focus indicator.[^12]

A practical case study is the modern modal dialog. Android's official design guidance explicitly uses full-screen and partial scrims to obscure backgrounds behind disruptive elements and transitions, while WCAG warns that overlapping layers, sticky footers, or semi-opaque effects can obscure focus or fail contrast requirements. The same technique therefore functions as helpful masking when it reduces background competition, and as harmful masking when it suppresses access to the active control pathway.[^13]

Audio and speech. In audio, presentation aims to increase intelligibility, separability, and perceived quality for intended listeners. ANSI's Speech Intelligibility Index defines a physical measure highly correlated with speech intelligibility and computed from acoustical measurements of speech and noise. Cherry's work on the cocktail-party problem showed that recognition improves when competing speech is separated by spatial direction, sex, pitch range, or speaking style, and Brungart's work on simultaneous talkers formalized the distinction between energetic masking and informational masking, which is crucial because not all speech interference is merely about energy overlap. Clear-speech research adds a presentation-side case study: clear speech is more intelligible than conversational speech in noise and reverberation, with one widely cited study reporting average gains of about 20 percentage points for normal-hearing listeners and 26 points for hearing-impaired listeners.[^14]

Masking in audio is both a perceptual phenomenon and an engineering tool. Classic work on auditory masking established that the threshold for one sound is changed by another sound, and later codec design exploited that phenomenon: MPEG audio standards and later MP3/AAC systems use psychoacoustic masking thresholds to remove or coarsely code acoustically irrelevant components while preserving perceived quality. Review articles on perceptual audio coding describe psychoacoustic models as delivering masking thresholds that quantify the maximum distortion likely to remain inaudible. This is a pure instance of masking as selective discard without salient perceptual loss.[^15]

A second audio case study is office sound masking. Contemporary acoustic studies describe sound masking as the addition of controlled broadband noise to reduce the distraction and intelligibility of ambient speech in open-plan offices. In one 2018 JASA study, speech-shaped stationary masking noise produced similar error rates at 3 dB higher speech-to-noise ratios than a more conventional masking spectrum, implying more efficient reduction of distracting-speech effects. In room and office privacy practice, metrics such as STI, distraction distance, and SPC are used to determine whether speech remains audible or intelligible outside the source area; Bradley and Gover describe SPC as combining outside-room noise level with transmitted speech level difference to estimate privacy.[^16]

The main trade-offs in audio are especially concrete. Better presentation can require higher level, clearer articulation, or more bandwidth, but each can interfere with comfort, naturalness, or privacy. Better masking can reduce distraction or protect confidentiality, but over-masking raises annoyance, fatigue, or loss of environmental awareness. Codec masking saves bitrate, but if the psychoacoustic model fails, the listener hears artifacts rather than "transparent" compression.[^17]

User interfaces. In UI work, presentation is not only visual styling; it is the choreography of discoverability, semantic structure, progressive complexity, and input reliability. W3C's disclosure pattern defines a disclosure as a widget that can collapse or expand content, with a button controlling content visibility. WCAG requires that information and relationships conveyed through presentation remain programmatically determinable, and its newer focus-obscuration criterion requires that focused components not be entirely hidden by author-created content. NIST's digital identity guidance adds an especially important usability-security layer for authentication interfaces by requiring support for password managers and recommending autofill, copy/paste support, and long passphrases.[^18]

Masking in UI often involves secure text entry, selective hiding, preview suppression, and progressive disclosure. Apple's Human Interface Guidelines describe secure text fields as the pattern to use when privacy is important, and Material Design's accessibility guidance explicitly recommends that password-reveal controls expose an accessible action label such as "Show password." This is a subtle but important point: the UI masks the content, yet the control that changes masking state must itself be clearly presented and accessible. That is the essence of well-designed selective access.[^19]

A practical UI case is the password field. Older "always masked" flows treated concealment as primary, but current official guidance shifts toward a more balanced model: allow autofill and paste, support long secrets, provide secure entry, and use clear accessible labeling for reveal controls. The lesson is that security masking that increases entry errors, blocks password managers, or hides state from assistive technologies is often a net failure because it worsens both usability and actual security outcomes. NIST's prohibition on displaying certain secrets on locked devices makes the same point from the other direction: some details genuinely should remain masked until the user is re-authenticated.[^20]

Data privacy. In privacy engineering, "masking" is the most overloaded term in the entire comparison. NIST IR 8053 describes de-identification of structured data as often involving removing, masking, or altering fields such as names and phone numbers, but NIST SP 800-188 is explicit that not all tools that merely mask personal information provide sufficient functionality for performing de-identification. SP 800-188 also defines de-identification as a general term for removing the association between identifying data and the data subject, emphasizes governance through Disclosure Review Boards and re-identification studies, and ties privacy controls to expected post-transformation identifiability and harm.[^21]

The primary theoretical distinction in privacy is between presentation-preserving transformation and identity-risk reduction. Format-preserving encryption is explicitly about preserving format while encrypting value; NIST SP 800-38G specifies FF1 and FF3 for that purpose. Tokenization similarly replaces the PAN with a surrogate token, and PCI guidance states that the security objective is for the resulting token to have no value to an attacker. These are useful controls, but they are not automatically equivalent to irreversible anonymization. They preserve operational presentation while masking sensitive content.[^22]

For de-identification proper, official U.S. guidance is clear. HHS states that HIPAA offers two methods—Expert Determination and Safe Harbor—and lists the identifiers that Safe Harbor removes. HHS also states that both methods, even when properly applied, leave some nonzero identification risk and may reduce downstream usefulness. Classic privacy research adds the formal layer: Sweeney's k-anonymity requires each record to be indistinguishable from at least k−1 others with respect to quasi-identifiers, while Dwork's differential privacy defines a randomized algorithm in which output probabilities change only within a bounded factor when any one record is added or removed. The U.S. Census Bureau's 2020 disclosure-avoidance modernization operationalizes that approach by applying noise within a differential-privacy framework, and it publicly tracks both privacy-protected demonstration data and quality metrics.[^23]

A real-world privacy case study is therefore the contrast between field masking and risk-based release design. Field masking may hide a Social Security number or name, but SP 800-188 warns that merely masking personal information may still be inadequate. By contrast, HIPAA expert determination, k-anonymity-style quasi-identifier control, and differential privacy explicitly reason about residual inference risk. The practical consequence is that privacy work must be evaluated not by whether fields "look hidden," but by whether the release is safe against plausible linkage and inference attacks relative to the release context.[^24]

Social and psychological contexts. Here, presentation generally means self-presentation, impression management, and role performance. Goffman's dramaturgical account treats everyday interaction as a staged social process, and Snyder's self-monitoring work operationalized the degree to which individuals regulate expressive behavior in response to social cues. On this side of the comparison, presentation is the selective amplification of socially useful cues: competence, warmth, status, solidarity, calm, or professionalism.[^25]

Masking in the social-psychological sense includes emotion masking, surface acting, deception control, stigma management, and camouflaging. Ekman and Friesen's work on nonverbal leakage highlights the instability of purely strategic concealment: people often fail to fully suppress cues. Emotional-labor research sharpened the distinction between surface acting and deep acting; Grandey's influential study tested surface acting as modification of outward expression and deep acting as modification of inner feeling, linking them to stress and coworker-rated affective delivery. Brotheridge and Lee's Emotional Labour Scale operationalized surface and deep acting as measurable workplace constructs.[^26]

A particularly important contemporary case is autistic camouflaging. The CAT-Q was developed to measure compensation, masking, and assimilation, and the original validation reported positive correlations with depression and anxiety and negative correlations with well-being and self-esteem. This does not mean all masking is pathological; in many settings it is adaptive, protective, or socially required. But it does mean the costs are real and measurable. More broadly, later reviews of emotional labor have consistently argued that surface acting more often predicts burnout or exhaustion, whereas deep acting is more often associated with performance or neutralized costs, though context matters.[^27]

The ethical stakes are highest in this domain. In design, privacy, and acoustics, masking is often evaluated instrumentally. In social life, masking also touches identity, coercion, authenticity, inclusion, and mental health. A mask can be a strategy of care, a tactic of survival, a job requirement, or a symptom of hostile environments. That ambiguity is why social masking cannot be assessed only by outward "effectiveness"; it must also be assessed by cumulative human cost.[^28]

## Measurement and evaluation

A rigorous evaluation starts by specifying which observer is supposed to succeed or fail. In visual presentation, the intended observer should extract structure accurately and quickly, so metrics include contrast ratios, task completion time, chart-reading error, and recovery of semantics from markup or text alternatives. Cleveland and McGill's work is the classic example of using quantitative judgment error to compare presentation formats, while WCAG converts accessible presentation into testable criteria such as contrast and semantic recoverability. In visual masking, by contrast, the benchmark is often reduced target visibility, elevated detection threshold, longer response time, or lower discrimination accuracy under controlled timing conditions.[^29]

In audio and speech, the measurement family is broader but conceptually similar. For presentation, SII and STI estimate intelligibility from signal and noise conditions; direct listening tests, word recognition, or sentence intelligibility measure realized understanding. For masking and privacy, acousticians often use SNR, STI, distraction distance, and SPC. In office studies, subjective annoyance and cognitive-performance tasks such as serial recall are also standard, because a masking system that technically reduces intelligibility but substantially raises annoyance may still be a design failure.[^30]

In user interfaces, measurement must cover both usability and accessibility. A masked password flow should be evaluated through completion rates, error or reset rates, support for password managers and paste, keyboard-access behavior, screen-reader labeling, and focus visibility under overlays or disclosures. W3C and NIST are useful here because they turn what is often treated as "mere UX preference" into concrete requirements and testable failure modes.[^31]

In data privacy, the minimum serious evaluation includes residual identifiability, attack model, utility, and governance. Typical metrics include k in k-anonymity; ε in differential privacy; suppression or generalization rates; distortion or query error; fit-for-use quality metrics; and empirical re-identification studies or simulated linkage attempts. SP 800-188 is especially useful because it frames acceptable controls relative to post-transformation identifiability and expected harm, not only relative to whether fields were altered.[^32]

In social and psychological settings, evaluation has to be longitudinal enough to capture hidden cost. The relevant question is not just whether the person "successfully presented" or "successfully masked," but whether the strategy predicts healthier adaptation or cumulative strain. Here the Self-Monitoring Scale, Emotional Labour Scale, CAT-Q, stress measures, burnout indices, anxiety and depression measures, and self-esteem or well-being scales are appropriate. The same outwardly effective behavior can therefore score as protective in one setting and damaging in another.[^33]

## Best practices and pitfalls

The strongest best practice across domains is to define the observer model and threat model first. If the intended observer is a driver, keyboard user, screen-reader user, office worker, analyst, or stigmatized employee, the right presentation or masking strategy changes immediately. WCAG, NIST de-identification guidance, and acoustic privacy standards all implicitly follow this idea by tying design choices to user task, expected harm, and measurable performance rather than to aesthetics alone.[^34]

A second best practice is to distinguish protective masking from mere hiding. In privacy, hiding a few obvious identifiers is not the same as de-identification. In UI, a collapsed panel or masked password is not automatically safe or usable. In social settings, polite self-presentation is not interchangeable with chronic suppression. Functions that constrain access should therefore be justified by explicit benefit: reduced re-identification risk, reduced shoulder-surfing, reduced distraction, or reduced stigma exposure.[^35]

A third best practice is to preserve semantic access even when surface access is controlled. Secure text entry can still expose actionable labels and reveal toggles. A disclosure can hide content but still communicate state and control relationships. A scrim can de-emphasize a background without hiding focus. A token can preserve format for workflows while removing direct disclosure value. This is where sophisticated systems outperform crude suppression: they separate operational utility from unwanted exposure.[^36]

A fourth best practice is to measure secondary effects, not just the primary design goal. Better chart salience can increase clutter; more office masking can increase annoyance; more privacy can reduce analytic utility; more emotional masking can increase exhaustion. The Renz office study, HIPAA's warning about information loss, and camouflaging research all show that success on the primary objective does not eliminate cost elsewhere.[^37]

The most common pitfall in visual and UI design is equating subtlety with sophistication. Low contrast, translucent overlays, hidden advanced options, or modal layers may look "clean" while materially reducing comprehension or accessibility. Official guidance is unusually explicit on this point: contrast has minimum thresholds; focus cannot be entirely obscured; and structural meaning cannot live only in visual formatting.[^38]

The biggest pitfall in privacy is conflating reversible masking, operational redaction, and formal de-identification. Format-preserving encryption and tokenization can be excellent operational controls, but they are not the same as safe public release. Field suppression can satisfy a local requirement while failing under linkage. Good privacy practice therefore combines technical transformation, expert review, governance, and empirical attack testing.[^39]

The most important pitfall in social contexts is rewarding successful masking while ignoring cost. Surface acting can improve immediate service presentation, and camouflaging can improve short-term social fit, yet both have documented links to strain-related outcomes. Systems that depend on such masking should therefore be judged not only by visible harmony but by whether they create conditions in which constant concealment becomes necessary.[^40]

Assumptions used in this report. "Presentation" is treated broadly as any intentional rendering of information, signal, or identity for perception and action, not merely slideshow delivery or public speaking. "Masking" is treated broadly enough to include perceptual masking, interface hiding, privacy transformation, and social self-concealment, while still distinguishing among them by mechanism and observer. In the UI section, "masking" includes both secure entry and selective disclosure patterns; in the privacy section, reversible operational controls such as tokenization or format-preserving encryption are treated separately from de-identification and anonymization.

Open questions and limitations. This is a cross-domain synthesis, so effect sizes are not directly comparable across domains because the units differ: contrast ratios, psychophysical thresholds, intelligibility indexes, ε values, and well-being scales do not live on a common scale. Several product-guideline sources, especially platform UI guidance, are versioned and may evolve faster than foundational research. Finally, the ethics discussion is necessarily more interpretive than the standards and psychophysics sections, even though it is grounded in primary social-science constructs and validated measures.[^47]
